Shasta County (CA) Wildfire Risk Assessment

ArcGIS Pro, AecGIS Online3 weeks

Mapped residential buildings in Shasta County's Wildland-Urban Interface to quantify wildfire exposure — identifying 16,746 at-risk buildings and roughly $7.54B in potential economic exposure.

▸ Read the sample case study

This project used GIS to spatially map residential buildings in Shasta County, California falling within the Wildland-Urban Interface (WUI): the zone where human development meets wildland vegetation — to provide a spatial evidence base for fire risk management and land-use planning.

Wildfire incidents across California — Shasta County highlighted (1:5,000,000, WGS 1984 Web Mercator).

Methodology

  • Clipped wildfire incidents, building footprints and land cover to the Shasta County boundary.
  • Extracted forest regions (deciduous, evergreen, mixed) from the land cover layer.
  • Created a 100m buffer around forested areas to define the WUI zone.
  • Selected residential buildings within the buffer and costed exposure at $450,000 per building.

Fire Risk Assessment Map — buildings within the 100m WUI zone against forest cover and wildfire incidents.

Results

16,746 residential buildings sit within the WUI. At an estimated $450,000 average property value, total potential economic exposure is approximately $7.54 billion — concentrated along forest edges in the northern and western county.

Key outcomes

  • Identified 16,746 buildings within the 100m WUI zone.
  • Quantified ~$7.54B in potential economic loss exposure.
  • Mapped spatial clustering of at-risk buildings along forest edges.

Digitised Map of West Wittering, West Sussex (1846)

ArcGIS Pro, ArcGIS Online2 weeks

Georeferenced and digitised an 1846 tithe map into a fully attributed GIS dataset — revealing land use, rent value and ownership patterns across a historic coastal parish.

▸ Read the sample case study

This project digitised the 1846 tithe map of West Wittering, West Sussex — around 150 hectares of beach, farmland and the sand spit at the entrance to Chichester Harbour — into a fully attributed GIS dataset.

Methodology

The map was georeferenced to the British National Grid, field boundaries were digitised as lines with topology checks, closed polygons were constructed, and the tithe apportionment table (land use, ownership, rent charge per acre) was joined to the polygons via a relational join.

Digitised land use — arable, pasture, meadow, buildings and coastal features (1:7,000, BNG).

Left: land use composition — arable (30.4%) and buildings (25.2%) dominate. Right: rent charge per acre, highest in the central farmland.

Land ownership

Gorham held the largest single estate at ~370 acres, followed by Whitby's Charity at ~145 acres — a concentrated pattern typical of Victorian landownership.

Key outcomes

  • Produced a georeferenced, topologically correct GIS dataset from the 1846 tithe map.
  • Classified land use across 150 hectares — arable (30.4%) the dominant category.
  • Linked rent value to soil productivity and revealed ownership concentration.

GIS Analysis · Flood Risk

River Medway — Nature-Based Solutions Suitability

ArcGIS Pro, ModelBuilder, OS Terrain 503 weeks

Multi-criteria GIS analysis identifying where to site nature-based flood interventions across the Medway catchment, delivering an 884.79 ha implementation portfolio within a £1.5M budget.

▸ Read the sample case study

An ArcGIS Pro ModelBuilder workflow combined a DEM, land cover, flood zone, soil infiltration and drainage-area data to score suitability for four Nature-Based Solutions: Runoff Attenuation Features, Floodplain Reconnection, Large Woody Debris, and Wet Woodland.

NbS suitability across the Medway catchment — RAF, LWD, FR and WW distribution (1:300,000, BNG).

Budget optimisation

Initial combined costs of £1.53M marginally exceeded budget. Overlapping suitability zones were resolved by keeping the more suitable, lower-cost option at each site, bringing the final portfolio to 884.79 ha at £1.32M.

Key outcomes

  • 884.79 ha of suitable land identified across four NbS types.
  • Delivered within budget at £1,321,124 total cost.
  • Floodplain Reconnection (288.32 ha) was the largest single intervention.
  • Fully repeatable ModelBuilder workflow, scalable to other catchments.

Broadway Gas Pipeline — Terrain Evaluation

ArcGIS Pro, QGIS, LiDAR, Digimap2 months

A geohazard terrain evaluation for a proposed gas pipeline through the Cotswold Escarpment — combining geology, LiDAR and field survey to recommend a route clear of active landslide zones.

▸ Read the sample case study

Broadway sits on alternating Jurassic limestone and mudstone — a geology that drives slope instability across the escarpment. The study combined geological desk study, aerial photo interpretation, LiDAR and a three-day field survey to map hazards and recommend a safe pipeline alignment.

LiDAR hillshade showing rotational landslides, solifluction lobes and translational landslides (1:10,000, Digimap).

Risk assessment

Rotational and overlapping multi-phase landslides were rated Very High risk, translational landslides and gullies High risk, and solifluction lobes Moderate risk — each assessed for its potential to bend or expose buried pipeline.

Recommended alignment — routed to avoid high-risk landslide zones and steep escarpment terrain (1:17,000, BNG).

Key outcomes

  • Mapped active, suspended and relict landslides across the escarpment.
  • Identified the mudstone/limestone contact as the primary driver of instability.
  • Recommended a route avoiding Very High and High risk terrain while staying constructable.

GIS Analysis · Remote Sensing

Sullington Warren — Digital Terrain Modelling

ArcGIS Pro, GNSS, Total Station, UAV6 weeks

Compared Kriging, IDW and Natural Neighbour interpolation for modelling a Bronze Age barrow cemetery — Kriging came out on top, with the tightest residual accuracy against GNSS checkpoints.

▸ Read the full case study

Elevation data for this National Trust heathland site were collected via GNSS, total station, MultiStation and UAV photogrammetry, then interpolated three ways to compare surface accuracy for archaeological terrain modelling.

Kriging DEM — the smoothest, most continuous surface of the three methods tested.

Residual analysis

Against seven GNSS checkpoints, Kriging produced the tightest error range (−0.039m to +0.229m, StdDev 0.093m), Natural Neighbour was moderate (StdDev 0.128m), and IDW showed the widest spread (StdDev 0.238m) due to distortion around clustered points.

Key outcomes

  • Kriging recommended as the preferred method for future archaeological terrain analysis at the site.
  • IDW's largest residual error (+0.546m) traced to uneven point clustering.
  • Multi-instrument survey approach gave comprehensive coverage of complex heathland terrain.
